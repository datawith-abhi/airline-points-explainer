Your airline can make money from you while you're buying groceries. No boarding pass required. The secret is a second business hiding behind the planes: selling reward points to banks. Seats are expensive to sell. Fuel, crews, aircraft, maintenance, and airports all need paying before a flight turns a profit. A bank buys miles from the airline, then awards them to customers for spending on its credit card. Swipe for coffee. Pay for groceries. Collect points. The bank sends cash to the airline, often before you take a trip. Banks want your business too. Cards can bring merchant fees, annual fees, and interest. Rewards make the card attractive. In twenty twenty-five, Delta received eight point two billion dollars from American Express through their partnership. That's a lot of everyday spending. But that cash is not all profit. Part covers future flights. Other portions pay for marketing and benefits. Filling an otherwise empty seat may cost less than the bank paid for the points. That's the economic appeal. But rewards aren't free. A full flight can mean displacing a paying passenger. Partner airlines also charge for award seats. Some points are never redeemed. Airlines estimate that breakage. Changing award prices can also change what your balance buys. So, more money from points than seats? The headline needs care. Partnership cash, ticket revenue, and profit are different measures. Ticket sales can still be much larger. The real story is how loyalty turns everyday spending into valuable airline cash. The plane makes the reward desirable. The card keeps cash flowing. You're part of the airline business, even on the ground.
